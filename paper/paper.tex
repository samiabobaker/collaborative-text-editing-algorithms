\begin{abstract}
    Collaborative text-editing algorithms, which are used in popular software such as Google Docs and Overleaf, have a troubled history: many published algorithms violate their purported consistency guarantees, and many algorithms' claimed properties have not been independently validated, making it unclear which algorithms are safe to use.
    Formal specifications that define what it means for a collaborative text-editing algorithm to be correct have been proposed recently, but few algorithms have been checked against these specifications, and to date there is no executable checker for them. 
    In this paper we introduce Darner, the first model checker specialised for finding violations of these specifications, supporting both randomised and exhaustive search.
    We also implement a corpus of over 50 such algorithms, covering most of the research literature and popular open-source libraries.
    Applying Darner to this corpus reveals that 17 of these algorithms do not meet the requirements that we consider essential for a collaborative text-editing algorithm to be practically useful; of these, 10 reproduce known results and 7 reveal flaws not reported previously.
    By providing the most comprehensive survey and empirical validation to date, we hope that this paper will place the field of collaborative text editing on a more stable footing going forward.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Collaborative text-editing algorithms allow multiple users on different machines to edit the same text document concurrently; they are used in popular software such as Google Docs and Overleaf.
Since Ellis and Gibbs' seminal paper on Operational Transformation (OT) in 1989~\cite{Ellis1989}, dozens of such algorithms have been published in papers or open-source implementations, and new algorithms have continued to appear in recent years.
Unfortunately, the field has been bedevilled by persistent correctness issues.
Not only was the original OT paper wrong, but several subsequent attempts to fix it were also shown to simply not work \cite{Oster2005Proving}, despite having been published in peer-reviewed venues. 
Many algorithms have been published without proof of their correctness, and some that were published with proofs also later turned out to be incorrect due to mistakes in the proofs \cite{Oster2005Proving}.

To add to the confusion, for decades there was no formal specification of what it means for a collaborative text editing algorithm to be correct.
The main property algorithms focussed on was convergence (i.e., all replicas eventually end up in the same state), but convergence alone is not sufficient to be useful: for example, a text editor could drop or reorder characters, resulting in garbled text, while still converging.
The CCI model from 1996 \cite{Sun1996} relies on a notion of ``intention preservation'' that is too vague to be checkable.
It was only in 2016 that Attiya et al.~\cite{Attiya2016} proposed the first formal specification of collaborative text editing, which in 2025 was extended with a further important property, non-interleaving \cite{Weidner2025Fugue}.
However, executable checkers for these specifications are not available, and only a few algorithms have been evaluated against these formal specifications so far.
As a result, there is a large collection of algorithms for collaborative text editing, historical and new, whose correctness properties are unknown.

This problem is exacerbated by the fact that for many algorithms, no implementation is publicly available, and the descriptions of the algorithms in papers are sometimes complex, vague, and/or incomplete, making it difficult to independently check them for correctness.
As a result, some work in this field has gained a reputation for being full of errors, too complicated, and not worth trying to understand \cite{Gentle2011,Levien2019}.

To address this situation, this paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce Darner,\footnote{Named after a family of dragonflies that hunts bugs in flight, and also after a darning needle for mending holes in textiles.} a specialised model checker for testing collaborative text editing algorithms, which includes checks for five properties: convergence, Attiya et al.'s weak and strong list specifications \cite{Attiya2016}, forward non-interleaving, and maximal non-interleaving \cite{Weidner2025Fugue}.
    \item We implement a corpus of over 50 collaborative text editing algorithms covering most of the research literature and open-source libraries, and use Darner to test these algorithms against the aforementioned properties.
        For many of these algorithms, this is the first publicly available implementation, and the first time their properties have been independently checked.
        Our results provide, for the first time, a comprehensive survey of the collaborative text editing literature from a correctness point of view, and for several published algorithms we find novel bugs that have not been reported previously.
    \item We evaluate Darner based on its ability to reproduce known results, and we compare its performance in an exhaustive checking mode with a randomised mode that probabilistically searches for violations of consistency properties.
\end{itemize}

As with any model checker, Darner can only explore a finite state space to establish either that an algorithm does not satisfy some property, or that it satisfies the property within bounded executions.
Making any guarantees about unbounded executions would require a formal proof.
However, the advantage of Darner is that adding new algorithms and checking them against our properties is quick and easy, making it possible to quickly rule out algorithms that are obviously broken.
We hope that Darner and our algorithm corpus will therefore become a standard part of future research on collaborative editing algorithms, allowing formal verification efforts to focus on those algorithms that have been shown by Darner to be plausible.

The scope of this paper is focussed on the correctness of collaborative text editing with single-character insertions and deletions, and it includes both the OT and the CRDT family of algorithms.
We leave an evaluation of algorithm performance and additional features (such as multi-character operations \cite{Lv2016}, undo \cite{Ressel1999}, move \cite{Kleppmann2020}, and rich text \cite{Litt2022}) for future work.
Similarly, the use of these algorithms for file types other than plain text (such as graphics editing \cite{Sun2002}, JSON \cite{Kleppmann2017}, and XML \cite{Davis2002}) is beyond the scope of this paper.


\section{Background} \label{sec:background}

\subsection{Introduction to collaborative text editing}

The goal of a collaborative text-editing algorithm is to allow several users to edit a shared text document in real time. When a user makes a change to the
document, network messages are sent to their collaborators so that they also see this change. We consider two types of changes: $\mathit{ins}(x, i)$ means the character $x$ is inserted in the document at index $i$, and $\mathit{del}(i)$ means the character at index $i$ is deleted.

These algorithms must have a responsive user interface even if the network is slow. Thus, when a user makes a change, it should be applied instantly to their local copy of the document, without waiting for any network round-trips. When a client receives a message about another client's change, the state of their document may be different from the document on which those changes were originally performed.
In particular, an index may now refer to a different position in the text.
The main difficulty of collaborative text editing is ensuring that clients nevertheless converge to a valid state.

There are two general approaches to solving this problem: Operational Transformation (OT) \cite{Ellis1989} and Conflict-free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs) \cite{Preguica2009}. We describe OT in more detail in \autoref{sec:otbackground}. A CRDT is a data structure specially designed to handle changes being made concurrently. The operations are represented in such a way that concurrent operations commute, i.e.\ they have the same effect regardless of the order in which they are executed.

Another way to categorise these algorithms is by the assumptions they make about the network. Some algorithms assume the existence of a server, and some are decentralised. Generally, the logic becomes simpler when there is a server, but it is a single point of failure, and avoiding a server may be preferred in some cases, such as peer-to-peer networks. Another assumption some algorithms make is that all clients are online, and the algorithm stops working if one client is disconnected from the rest of the network. This is a much more drastic assumption to make than assuming a server exists, since now every device is a single point of failure. 

\subsection{Defining the correctness of an algorithm}

The CCI model \cite{Sun1996} was an early proposal aiming to define what it means for a collaborative text editing algorithm to be correct.
The three properties it describes are convergence, causality preservation, and intention preservation. Convergence means that all clients eventually reach the same document state, once all network messages have been received. Causality preservation means that if 
an operation $O_b$ is causally dependent on $O_a$, then $O_a$ must be executed before $O_b$ on all clients. Intention preservation says that when an operation is executed, the intention
of the user must be preserved.
While the first two properties are well-defined, the meaning of ``intention'' in this model is unclear, making it impossible to formally check compliance with the CCI model.

\subsubsection{Weak and Strong List Specifications}

The weak and strong list specifications of Attiya et al.~\cite{Attiya2016} are precise correctness properties for collaborative text editing (or, more generally, a list object supporting insertion and deletion at arbitrary positions) that go beyond convergence and causality preservation.
We use these properties in Darner.

The weak and strong list specifications describe which characters must appear in the document, and constrain their order, at every point in the execution.
Firstly, a character must appear in the document if and only if an insertion operation
of it has been seen, and a deletion operation has not been seen. Secondly, if a character $a$ appears before a character $b$ in some client's document state, $a$ must appear before $b$ in every client's document
state.

The difference between the two is that the weak list specification is more relaxed about the ordering relative to deleted characters.
For example, say a document initially reads $x$, and then three clients concurrently modify it: the first inserts $a$ before $x$ to produce $ax$, the second inserts $b$ after $x$ to produce $xb$, and the third deletes $x$ to produce an empty document.
When these edits are merged, the weak list specification allows the final state to be either $ab$ or $ba$, whereas the strong list specification allows only $ab$.

We quote the relevant parts of these specifications here, but refer to the original paper \cite{Attiya2016}
for the full properties.



\begin{property}[Strong List Specification]\label{prop:stronglist}
  There exists a binary relation $lo$ on the characters of the state, called the list order, such that:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item
      For all events $e = do(op, w)$, where $op$ is a locally executed operation that results in a sequence of elements $w = a_0 \dots a_{n-1}$, the following properties are satisfied:
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item
          $w$ contains exactly the elements visible to $e$ that have been inserted, but not deleted.
        \item
          The order of these elements is consistent with the list order:
          $\forall i,j. (i < j) \implies (a_i, a_j) \in lo$.
        \item
          Elements are inserted at the specified position: if $op = ins(a, k)$, then $a = a_{\min\{k, n-1\}}$.
      \end{enumerate}
    \item
      The list order $lo$ is transitive, irreflexive and total, and thus determines the order of all insert operations in the execution.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{property}


\begin{property}[Weak List Specification]
  There exists a binary relation $lo$ on the characters of the state, called the list order, such that:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item
      Same as strong list specification.
    \item
      The list order $lo$ is irreflexive and, for all events $e = do(op, w)$, it is transitive and total on $\{a \mid a \in w \}$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{property}

\subsubsection{Interleaving}

One limitation of the weak and strong list specifications is that they allow interleaving behaviour. When users concurrently insert text at the same position, some algorithms may interleave those changes
with each other, rather than keeping the new passages separate.
The weak and strong list specifications allow arbitrary interleaving, even though this can lead to unusable garbled text \cite{Kleppmann2019}.

Forward interleaving \cite{Weidner2025Fugue} occurs when both insertions are performed in the forward direction (typing ``cat'' by typing `c' then `a' then `t'), and the merged state exhibits interleaving.
Backward interleaving occurs when text is written in the backward direction (typing ``cat'' by typing `t' then `a' then `c'), and the merged state is interleaved.
Since backward insertions happen rarely in typical text editing behaviour, backward interleaving is not an important practical concern, but forward interleaving is.

It has recently been shown that it is impossible for an algorithm to guarantee both forward and backward non-interleaving \cite{Weidner2025Fugue}.
Maximal non-interleaving is a strengthening of forward non-interleaving that also disallows backward interleaving when possible, but which prioritises forward non-interleaving in cases where it is not possible to prevent both interleaving directions.

In this paper we check for forward non-interleaving as well as maximal non-interleaving, and to our knowledge Darner is the first implementation of these properties.
We expect that an algorithm that satisfies forward non-interleaving but not maximal non-interleaving is good enough for most practical applications, but we report the results for maximal non-interleaving as well because backward insertion may be more frequent in some applications.

The properties assume the algorithm uses \emph{tombstones}. That is, rather than actually deleting an element from the document state, the algorithm simply marks the element as deleted. Marked elements are called tombstones, and are not displayed to the user when outputting the document state.

We quote the relevant properties below, but refer to the Fugue paper \cite{Weidner2025Fugue} for the full details.

\begin{definition}[Left Origin]
  The left origin of an element is the element directly preceding its insertion position at the time of insertion. If the element was inserted by 
  an $ins(x, i)$ call, then its left origin was at index $i-1$ immediately prior to this call. If $i=0$, then its left origin is a special symbol
  $\mathit{start}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Right Origin]
  The right origin of an element is the element directly following its insertion position at the time of insertion, in the list including tombstones. If the element was inserted by 
  an $ins(x, i)$ call, then its right origin was at index $i$ immediately prior to this call. If the insertion is at the end of the list, then its right origin is a special symbol
  $\mathit{end}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{property}[Forward non-interleaving]
  An algorithm is forward non-interleaving if it satisfies the strong list specification and the following holds for all list elements $A$ and $B$ in all possible list
  states: if $A$ is the left origin of $B$, and $B$ appears earlier in the list than any other element that has $A$ as left origin, then $A$ and $B$ are consecutive list
  elements.
\end{property}

\begin{property}[Maximal non-interleaving]
  An algorithm is maximal non-interleaving if it satisfies the strong list specification and the following holds for all list elements $A$ and $B$ in all possible states:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item
      (Forward non-interleaving) If $A$ is the left origin of $B$, and $B$ appears earlier in the list than any other element that has $A$ as left origin, then $A$ and $B$ 
      are consecutive list elements.
    \item
      (Backward non-interleaving) If $B$ is the right origin of $A$, and $A$ appears later in the list than any other element that has $B$ as right origin, then $A$ and $B$ are
      consecutive list elements. There is an exceptional case where this does not need to be true (see the original paper).
    \item
      If $A$ and $B$ have the same left origin and the same right origin, then the element with the lower ID appears earlier in the list.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{property}

\section{Operational Transformation Background} \label{sec:otbackground}

This section gives an overview of the common terminology and properties used by OT algorithms.

The idea of OT is to transform incoming remote operations with respect to concurrent operations that have already been applied locally. For example, if a client receives a
$del(3)$ operation, but it has already applied a concurrent $ins(x,2)$, then the original character has shifted one to the right so the deletion is now transformed to $del(4)$. The functions
that describe these transformations are called transformation functions. Transformation functions are paired with a control algorithm, which determines when and how to call these 
transformation functions. The intent is that the control algorithm makes certain assumptions about the properties of the transformation functions, and any functions that satisfy these properties
can be used with that algorithm.

\subsection{Inclusion Transformations}


The first kind of transformation function is the \emph{inclusion transformation}, which is used by all control algorithms. 

\begin{definition}[Inclusion Transformation]
  For two operations $O_1$ and $O_2$ generated from the same state, we write $O_1' = IT(O_1, O_2)$. $O_1'$ is the transformed operation
  of $O_1$, now taking into account the effects of $O_2$. For example, $del(4) = IT(del(3), ins(x, 2))$.
\end{definition}

There are two properties that have appeared in the literature for inclusion transformation functions, which are often required for the control algorithms to converge. These are TP1 and TP2~\cite{Ressel1996} (sometimes also called CP1 and CP2 respectively in the literature).

\begin{property}[TP1]
  If for all operations $O_1$ and $O_2$ based on the same document state we have $O_1' = IT(O_1, O_2)$, $O_2' = IT(O_2, O_1)$, and
  $O_2' \circ O_1 = O_1' \circ O_2$, then $IT$ satisfies TP1. That is, for all document states, executing $O_1$ followed by $O_2'$ results in the same
  document state as executing $O_2$ followed by $O_1'$.
\end{property}

TP1 is the basic property that ensures that using $IT$ will lead the clients to converge. However, when there are more than two clients and no server is assumed, some algorithms additionally require the TP2 property:

\begin{property}[TP2]
  If for all operations $O$, $O_1$, and $O_2$ based on the same document state we have $O_1' = IT(O_1, O_2)$, $O_2' = IT(O_2, O_1)$, and
  $IT(IT(O, O_1), O_2') = IT(IT(O, O_2), O_1')$, then $IT$ satisfies TP2.
\end{property}

Finding transformation functions that satisfy TP2 has been a challenge. Previous work has shown that TP2 cannot be satisfied without introducing additional parameters into the operations \cite{Randolph2015}. Several published functions claim to satisfy TP2, but were later shown not to \cite{Oster2005Proving}, or have not been independently checked
\cite{Randolph2015, Sun2017}. It is important to note that satisfying TP2 is only required for some control algorithms. An algorithm is \emph{TP2-avoiding}
if it does not assume TP2.

\subsection{Exclusion Transformations}


A few algorithms additionally use \emph{exclusion transformations}, which are the inverse of $IT$.

\begin{definition}[Exclusion Transformation]
  For two operations $O_1$ and $O_2$ where $O_2$ is generated from the resulting state of $O_1$, we write $O_2' = ET(O_2, O_1)$. $O_2'$ is the transformed operation of $O_2$,
  now excluding the effects of $O_1$.
\end{definition}


Exclusion transformations must satisfy the reversibility requirement, which enforces that $ET$ and $IT$ are inverses.

\begin{property}[Reversibility Requirement]
  $IT$ and $ET$ meet the reversibility requirement if, for all operations $O_1$ and $O_2$,
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $ET(IT(O_1, O_2), O_2) = O_1$
    \item $IT(ET(O_1, O_2), O_2) = O_1$
  \end{enumerate}
\end{property}


$ET$ is only relevant to a few algorithms. Nonetheless, there are transformation functions which claim to satisfy the reversibility requirements, but actually do not (see \autoref{sec:gotimplementation}).
Note that some papers use the terms \emph{forward} and \emph{backward transposition}, which roughly correspond to inclusion and exclusion transformations.

\section{Darner implementation} \label{sec:modelcheckerimplementation}

In this section, we describe our implementation of Darner. To our knowledge, this is the first automated checker for the weak and strong list specifications and for the non-interleaving properties. Both Darner and the algorithm implementations are written in Python, without the use of any existing model checking tool. Darner is about 2,300 lines of code, and the algorithm corpus is about 17,000 lines of code in total (averaging about 400 lines of code per algorithm).
We are releasing both Darner and its corpus as open source.\footnote{\url{\artifacturl}}

\subsection{Interface between algorithms and Darner}

We start by describing the interface between our algorithms and Darner, which determines the assumptions Darner makes about the algorithms.
Our implementation represents algorithms using the \texttt{ClientDevice} and \texttt{ServerDevice} (if the algorithm uses a server) interfaces. The interfaces provide
some mechanisms for Darner to interact with the devices.

The first is the method \texttt{perform\_operation}, which allows Darner to execute a \texttt{ClientOperation} or \texttt{ServerOperation}. These operations
include local edits to the document (such as \texttt{ClientInsertOperation} and \texttt{ClientDeleteOperation}), and also receiving operations from other devices
(such as \texttt{ClientReceiveFromClientOperation}). By including receiving operations, Darner has control over which messages are delivered to 
which device at which time, allowing it to model a wide range of network scenarios. Not all operations are relevant to all algorithms; for example, we include a \texttt{ClientTimestepOperation} that is only relevant to the TIBOT and TIBOT 2.0 algorithms.

The editing operations generated by the checker insert or delete a single character at a time. Support for multi-character operations (e.g.\ pasting a chunk of text) could be added in future work.

\texttt{perform\_operation} returns a list of operations seen by the client. This allows Darner to know what operations were contained in the message that the client received.

A second mechanism is provided by the methods \texttt{can\_receive\_from} and \texttt{can\_receive\_from\_server}. Algorithms tend to restrict the delivery order of operations. The most common way is to use a causal order, enforced by vector clocks. We leave the choice of restriction to the algorithm, and it is exposed to Darner through these methods, which return the devices from which a client can receive.

Finally, there is the method \texttt{read\_state}, which Darner can use to see the document state of the client. The state is represented by the type 
\texttt{list[UniqueChar]}. It is simply a list of unique characters. \texttt{UniqueChar} is just a character with a unique ID attached to it, which we use because many 
of the algorithms and specifications assume every character is unique.

\subsection{Generating operations}

Darner generally proceeds as follows. It first generates an operation to be executed on one of the clients or the server. It executes that operation, and records which operations were performed on which device, what the states of the clients were after each operation, and other useful information (for example, the left and right origins of each character). It repeats this with more operations to produce a trace of the execution. After the trace is fully generated, it is passed to one of the checkers which decides whether a particular property is satisfied.

The possible choices for an operation to be generated are an insertion or deletion at every position in the state, or receiving a message
from one of the other clients or server. We can then choose these operations at random, or check each one exhaustively up to a certain depth.
We implement both, but focus mainly on random generation.

The model checker does not generate operations for sending messages. When to send a message is left as a choice to the algorithm implementation. Most algorithms send a message after every local operation, but some (such as TIBOT) delay this.

\subsection{Checking for convergence}

Checking for convergence is very simple. The checker ensures that all messages have been delivered by having each client/server receive operations
until there is nothing left to receive. The checker then checks that all the document states of the clients are equal, and reports a violation if not.

\subsection{Checking for the strong list specification}

The strong list specification checker takes in a trace containing the state of each client after each operation. The trace also records which operations were visible
to each client at each point (which is determined by what is returned from \texttt{perform\_operation}). Darner can use this trace to check each condition given in Property \ref{prop:stronglist}.

Condition 1a is checked by maintaining an initially empty set of characters. For each client, Darner
iterates through each insertion or deletion seen by that client, including received operations. If it was an insertion operation, the inserted character is added to the set.
If it was a deletion operation, the deleted character is removed from the set. At each point, the checker tests whether this set is equal to the set of characters in the client state. If not, it reports a strong list specification violation.

Condition 1c is checked only for states following a local insertion operation. The checker tests whether the character at the position of insertion is equal
to the inserted character, and reports a violation if not.

The other conditions use the list order $\mathit{lo}$. The specification says there exists a list order, but does not specify how to construct it.
We do this by working backwards from the conditions imposed on the list order. Either the checker successfully constructs a list order, or it finds a contradiction showing that one cannot exist, and reports a strong list specification violation.

The list order is represented as a set of pairs of characters. It is initially empty. Working backwards from condition 1b, the checker adds to this set every pair of characters $(a,b)$ such that $a$ appears before $b$ in some document state. Working backwards from the requirement that $\mathit{lo}$ is transitive, the checker applies the transitive closure to the list order. The requirement that $\mathit{lo}$ is irreflexive is checked by iterating through the set of pairs and checking that there are no pairs of the form $(a,a)$. If there are, we have a contradiction showing that no list order satisfying these properties exists.

The only remaining part is that $\mathit{lo}$ must be a total relation. An irreflexive, transitive, and total relation can always be constructed from an irreflexive and transitive one by arbitrarily adding pairs to make the relation total without breaking irreflexivity. Therefore, if Darner has found an irreflexive and transitive relation, then it can say an irreflexive, transitive and total list order must also exist, and the specification is satisfied.

This approach is equivalent to constructing a graph where the nodes are characters in the state and an edge from $a$ to $b$ means that $a$ appears before $b$ in some state. If this graph is acyclic, then the strong list specification is satisfied. If it contains a cycle, it is not satisfied.

\subsection{Checking for the weak list specification}

Checking for the weak list specification is similar to the strong list, except that the list order only has to be transitive on the subsets of characters
$\{a \mid a \in w \}$ for each $w$, where $w$ is the state of some client after a local operation. This translates to having to compute the transitive closure once for each
state, and only on the characters in that state. As before, there is a single check for irreflexivity at the end.

\subsection{Issues with checking for non-interleaving}

The non-interleaving properties assume that the algorithms use tombstones, because the right origin may be a tombstone. We find this causes difficulties when the algorithm does not use tombstones, as the properties are not explicit in that case. It is not straightforward to add tombstones to an algorithm that does not use them, as the position of the tombstones is not well-defined. Further consideration may be needed as to what extent these properties can be tested
on those algorithms. We avoid these issues by generating only insertions when checking for interleaving, and omitting deletions. The behaviour of deletions is still checked in the weak and strong list specifications, so we consider this an acceptable omission.

Another reason for omitting deletions from the interleaving tests is that the non-interleaving properties are defined in terms of the total list order from the strong list specification.
For algorithms that satisfy the weak but not the strong list specification, this total order is not uniquely defined, and thus the question of whether interleaving is present is difficult to answer unambiguously.
In executions containing only insertions, there is no difference between the weak and strong list specifications, and thus the presence of interleaving can easily be determined from the final document state after all clients converge.

\subsection{Checking for non-interleaving}

The trace used in the non-interleaving checkers is the same as that for the weak and strong list specifications, but now includes the left and right origins of each character. These are straightforward to determine by simply checking the elements immediately before and after the character at the time of insertion. Since there are no deletions, it is not possible for the right origin to be a tombstone.

Knowing these, the condition for forward non-interleaving is easy to check. The checker loops over all pairs of characters $A$ and $B$. If $A$ is the left origin of $B$, and $B$ appears earlier in the list than all other characters for which $A$ is left origin, but $A$ and $B$ are not consecutive, then a forward non-interleaving
violation is reported.

For maximal non-interleaving, the condition for backward non-interleaving can be checked analogously. Maximal non-interleaving's third condition defines the ordering 
of characters with the same left and right origin; this was included to ensure the ordering is unique. It mentions an ID but does not prescribe what that ID is. We leave the choice of this ID to the algorithm; thus, algorithms that satisfy maximal non-interleaving still have the choice of ID as a degree of freedom over the ordering of characters. For that reason, we do not check condition 3
of maximal non-interleaving.  

\subsection{Checking properties of transformation functions}

In most of the OT literature, algorithms are split into two layers, control algorithms and transformation functions, which can be freely combined as long as the transformation functions satisfy the assumptions made by the control algorithm.
Some papers specify only a control algorithm but no transformation functions, or vice versa.
Prior work has focussed on checking the TP1 and TP2 properties of transformation functions \cite{Imine2003,Jungnickel2015}.

We depart from that tradition in this paper, and always test a control algorithm in conjunction with a particular set of transformation functions, because we found that the two cannot be separated from each other so cleanly.
The TP1 and TP2 properties are only relevant for ensuring convergence, but say nothing about whether an algorithm will satisfy the strong or weak list specification.
For example, we find that TM1.1 \cite{Sun2017} and Randolph et al.'s functions \cite{Randolph2015} both satisfy TP1 and TP2, but give different properties when tested with the same control algorithm.
In our experiments we therefore treat an OT algorithm as a pair of a control algorithm and transformation functions, sometimes testing the same control algorithm with several transformation functions, and vice versa.
We only test combinations where there is a match between the claimed properties of the transformation functions and the claimed assumptions of the control algorithm.

\section{The Darner algorithm corpus} \label{sec:modelcheckerresults}

To demonstrate the generality of Darner and its ability to find bugs, we implemented a corpus of collaborative text editing algorithms covering most research publications in this field, as well as a selection of popular open-source implementations.
Our implementations are not optimised for performance, but rather aim to be close to the published pseudocode, making it easy to check that our implementation correctly reflects the original published algorithm.
For any violation of a property found by Darner, we additionally check by hand that the violation does indeed occur in the algorithm as originally published.

In cases where an open-source implementation already exists (often as part of a larger library that also provides many other features), we develop our own implementation against Darner's API that focuses only on the collaborative text editing algorithm.
If Darner finds a violation of a property, we replay the same scenario against the original implementation to ensure the behaviour is consistent.

\subsection{Properties of each algorithm}

\autoref{tab:algorithmproperties} shows the list of algorithms we implemented in our corpus, and the results we found from model checking each algorithm using Darner.
The properties are: convergence, Attiya et al.'s weak and strong list specifications, and the forward and maximal non-interleaving properties (titled \emph{Forward} and \emph{Max}). It also includes which algorithms assume all clients are online (\emph{AllOnline}) --- see \autoref{sec:assumptions}. When there is a choice of transformation functions,
we choose transformation functions that would substantiate or disprove prior claims about the functions. For rows with no transformation functions, we used the transformation functions from the same paper as the control algorithm.
For space reasons, the property violations we found with specific algorithms are detailed in an appendix.

In our opinion, convergence and the weak list specification are minimal requirements for a collaborative text editing algorithm to be useful in practice.
Forward non-interleaving is also important, although algorithms differ on how frequently interleaving occurs in realistic editing scenarios (something we do not aim to quantify in this paper); an algorithm in which forward interleaving is rare but not impossible may still be acceptable in practice.
Whether an algorithm satisfies the strong list specification and/or maximal non-interleaving is less important in practice, but we include them in our analysis as they are well-defined properties of theoretical interest, and they could be important for some applications.

A common pattern we found across OT algorithms is that they exhibited interleaving in one direction, but not the other. Flipping the priority scheme flipped which direction of interleaving was exhibited. In these cases, we choose the direction that leads to forward non-interleaving being satisfied.

\subsection{Properties of transformation functions}

\autoref{tab:tfproperties} shows the properties of published transformation functions. It is important to note that these properties are only relevant to convergence, and make no guarantees about anything else. To determine these properties, we run Darner with the adOPTed algorithm, which is a TP2-requiring control algorithm. If no convergence violations are found on two clients, then we say the functions satisfy TP1. If no convergence violations are found on three or more clients, then we say they satisfy TP2. 

In addition to the properties shown in the table, we believe that the only functions that explicitly satisfy the reversibility requirements are the tombstone transformation functions \cite{Oster2006TTF}. The functions provided alongside GOT claim to satisfy the reversibility requirements, but we show that this is false.

\subsection{Novel property violations found}

Darner found the following violations of consistency properties that, to our knowledge, have not previously been reported in the literature (see Appendix \ref{sec:algorithmimplementations} for details):

\begin{itemize}
  \item
    The GOT algorithm \cite{Sun1998TOCHI}, claiming to be a decentralised TP2-avoiding algorithm, fails to converge with the transformation functions given in the same paper. The main cause is that the transformation functions fail to satisfy GOT's reversibility requirement.
  \item
    The TM1.1 transformation functions \cite{Sun2017} claim to satisfy TP2. We could not find any TP2 violations, as the functions converged with adOPTed. However, we found the functions led to weak list specification violations: in certain cases, the functions will delete an extra character that the user did not intend to delete.
  \item
    The algorithm used by the ProseMirror text editor \cite{Haverbeke2015} exhibits weak list specification violations. In certain cases, the algorithm can reorder characters without the user intending this.
  \item
    We found the LBT \cite{Li2005} algorithm did not converge. While its predecessor, SDT \cite{Li2008}, was already known not to work \cite{Oster2005Proving}, LBT had not been tested independently. In contrast, we could not find any convergence or weak or strong list specification violations for ABT \cite{Li2010}, which is LBT's successor.
  \item
    Partial persistent sequences \cite{Wu2010} represent the position of characters using rational numbers. The algorithm requires a mechanism for ensuring every character has a unique position, even if two clients concurrently insert a character at the same position. Two mechanisms to solve this issue have been proposed \cite{Wu2009, Wu2010}, but they do not solve the problem: the algorithm nevertheless exhibits convergence violations.
  \item
    We found convergence violations in Sync7 \cite{Little2024} and weak list specification violations in Diffsync \cite{Little2017}.
  \item
    We also found many strong list specification and interleaving violations in algorithms that were not explicitly checked previously. For example, Loro exhibits maximal non-interleaving violations despite its documentation listing this property as a supported feature (see \autoref{sec:loroimplementation}).
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Assumptions made by each algorithm}  \label{sec:assumptions}

Some algorithms assume the existence of a server that imposes a total order on all operations, requiring a client to communicate with the server before the operation can be processed by any other client.
For fault tolerance, the server can be replaced with a consensus protocol providing a replicated log, such as Raft \cite{Ongaro2014}.
Algorithms that make this assumption are listed in the category \emph{Total-order-based OT} in \autoref{tab:algorithmproperties}.
Some work \cite{Sun2020arxiv} differentiates between a transformation-based server and a non-transformation-based server, based on whether or not the server actually performs any transformations. In our opinion this is not a meaningful distinction, because any algorithm with a transformation-based server can be implemented with a non-transformation-based server, by having the server simply serialise the operations and the clients simulate what the server would do.  

Sometimes this assumption is made implicitly.
SOCT3 and SOCT4 use a sequencer, which is a non-transformation-based server.
Some TP2-avoiding algorithms do so by assuming totally ordered delivery, which has been proven to be equivalent to consensus \cite{Chandra1996}; we therefore place these algorithms in the same category as server-based algorithms.

Three algorithms we considered (SOCT4 \cite{Vidot2000}, TIBOT \cite{Li2004ICPADS}, and TIBOT 2.0 \cite{Xu2014}) even assume that all clients are online, and stop making progress if one or more clients become unavailable.
While a server-based algorithm can be made fault-tolerant using a consensus protocol, that is not the case for these algorithms, making them fundamentally unable to tolerate faults.

OT algorithms that do not assume a server or consensus protocol, as well as the CRDT algorithms studied in this paper, instead make a weaker assumption of \emph{causally ordered delivery}.
This model allows concurrent operations to be delivered in any order.
It can be efficiently implemented over peer-to-peer networks (e.g.\ using vector clocks), and even during network partitions it allows any users to continue collaborating if they are still able to communicate.

\section{Evaluation}

Because we only check a finite number of cases, rather than writing a proof, a \noviol{} in \autoref{tab:algorithmproperties} does not guarantee that the property is satisfied. The purpose of this evaluation is to give confidence that, if Darner is unable to find property violations in the algorithm, then it is unlikely that one exists. 

We use our large collection of algorithm implementations to collect data on the presence of violations and the time to find them, and to compare different search strategies. When running Darner, we have many choices over the configurations we use to search for violations. These include the number of traces generated, the number of operations per trace, the number of clients, whether to use random or exhaustive search, and what search order to use for an exhaustive search.
We use a seed to initialise a pseudorandom generator on each execution trace, which is used for all random choices, allowing us to reproduce each trace deterministically from that seed.
We investigate how the configuration affects the likelihood of finding a violation, and we justify our use of random checking over exhaustive checking.

\subsection{Reproducing known properties}

Darner was able to reproduce all published consistency properties of the algorithms tested, including the following violations, found within seconds using randomised testing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item
    Five transformation functions: Ellis \cite{Ellis1989}, Ressel \cite{Ressel1996}, Imine \cite{Imine2003}, Suleiman \cite{Suleiman1997} and SDT \cite{Li2008} were all shown not to satisfy TP2 \cite{Oster2005Proving} (and some do not satisfy TP1). We combined the first four with the adOPTed algorithm, and all produced convergence violations for $\ge 3$ clients (which is when TP2 is relevant). We also reproduced convergence violations in SDT using its own control algorithm.
  \item
    The dOPT algorithm is known not to converge \cite{Sun1996}.
  \item
    Fugue does not satisfy maximal non-interleaving \cite{Weidner2025Fugue}.
  \item
    Jupiter is known not to satisfy the strong list specification \cite{Attiya2016}.
  \item
    The Fugue paper \cite{Weidner2025Fugue} gives a list of algorithms for which the authors could find forward interleaving (e.g.\ Logoot, LSEQ, Treedoc, WOOT) and could not find forward interleaving (e.g.\ RGA, Yjs, Sync9). Our results are consistent with those (apart from when we were able to avoid forward interleaving by reversing the priority schemes, see \autoref{sec:doptjupiteradoptedimplementation}). 
  \item
    The Logoot algorithm is known to have flaws which lead it to get stuck in an infinite loop \cite{Sun2018}.
\end{itemize}


Similarly, when an algorithm is known to satisfy a particular property, Darner did not falsely report violations of that property. This includes Fugue \cite{Weidner2025Fugue} satisfying the strong list specification and forward non-interleaving, and FugueMax additionally satisfying maximal non-interleaving. Similarly, we found Jupiter satisfies the weak list specification, and RGA satisfies the strong list specification, which are both proven properties \cite{Wei2018, Attiya2016}.  


\subsection{How commonly violations appear}

\autoref{fig:violationrate} shows how the percentage of property-violating traces changes as a function of the number of operations per trace. This data is useful because it suggests what depth an exhaustive search would need to be run at; if we know a violation does not appear until a certain depth, then doing an exhaustive search below that depth will not find violations. Similarly, it suggests what number of operations to generate when doing a randomised search. We pick a set of algorithms for each property. There is no guarantee that these patterns will apply to every one of these algorithms, but these figures do illustrate some of the behaviours that are possible, and that we should consider when choosing a search strategy.

We observe that some of the violations (such as convergence violations in dOPT, or forward non-interleaving violations in TreeDoc) appear very commonly. The violations appear at fewer than 10 operations, and once the number of operations is large enough, violations appear nearly always.

Other violations (such as the strong list specification violations) do not appear as commonly. They do not appear at all when there are fewer than 6 operations, and only appear very rarely when there are fewer than 10 operations. Even with more than 10 operations, the violations are unlikely. Violating these properties requires a more specific combination of operations, so Darner does not find them as often. For example, a maximal non-interleaving violation (that is not also a forward non-interleaving violation) may require the trace to include both forward insertions and backward insertions. Running an exhaustive search with fewer than 10 operations per trace may miss these violations.

We also measured whether the number of clients changes how often violations appear. We found that some violations only appear when there are more than two clients (such as TP2 violations), but increasing the number of clients beyond three has no clear effect in most algorithms. An exception is PPS, where violations appeared only with more than 10 clients (see \autoref{sec:ppsimplementation}).

\subsection{What depth to use for exhaustive search}

Running an exhaustive search to that depth would take years of CPU time (see \autoref{fig:exhaustivesearchcost}), which exceeds our available resources. We therefore focus on randomised search.

\subsection{Comparing random and exhaustive search}

\autoref{fig:successrate} demonstrates the effectiveness of randomised search compared to exhaustive search. For all (algorithm, property) pairs for which we found a violation, we run a search and measure how long it takes to find the first violation. We find that randomised search finds a violation orders of magnitude faster than exhaustive search.
% NOTE: I think it's not correct to say that exhaustive search was only able to find a fraction of the violations -- if you had enough compute time, presumably it would be able to find all violations. It just takes much longer.

We considered multiple search strategies for exhaustive search: breadth-first vs.\ depth-first search, and deterministic vs.\ randomised (but still exhaustive) search order. The motive behind using a randomised exhaustive search is that a deterministic search order tends to search through many `uninteresting' cases first, such as all operations being on one client. This means that the search can spend a long time testing cases that are unlikely to trigger bugs. Randomised exhaustive search randomises the order of siblings in the search tree. The advantage of a depth-first search is that it is guaranteed to check a large number of operations, whereas a breadth-first search will spend a long time before reaching the maximum number of operations. The issue with a depth-first search is that it can spend a long time searching through subtrees that are not likely to contain any violations. We observe that none of these alterations produced a clear improvement in the success rate of the search.

  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{time_to_violation}

The Jupiter strong list specification violation is an example of a violation that random search found, but exhaustive search could not within a reasonable amount of time. There were no examples of violations that we could find through exhaustive search but not through randomised search. It may be worth considering in future work whether there are heuristics that would help a randomised or exhaustive search to find certain properties faster.

\section{Related work}


This section reviews the existing efforts to verify collaborative text-editing algorithms. None of these check as many algorithms as we do, and none check for either the strong list specification or the non-interleaving properties.


Imine et al.~\cite{Imine2003, Oster2005Proving, Imine2006} use the automated theorem prover SPIKE to verify TP1 and TP2 properties of various transformation functions, finding violations in five. The work only considers transformation functions in isolation, without any control algorithm. 
It therefore only establishes convergence, and not any of the other properties we check. 
% NOTE: commented out the following because it's a bit unfair -- a specification error can happen to anyone, and I don't think we would be immune to it.
% It also does not actually execute the functions, but makes deductions based on the provided specification. 
% This led to them falsely proving functions satisfied TP2; the same authors found the proof was incorrect because of an error in their specification. 
Boucheneb and Imine \cite{Boucheneb2009} also obtained similar results for convergence using the UPPAAL model checker.


Previous work has also used the TLC model checker and TLA+ specification language to verify various correctness properties. 
Oster et al.\ \cite{Oster2005WOOT} used this to verify convergence in the WOOT algorithm, exhaustively up to four clients and five characters. 
Wei et al.\ \cite{Wei2020} used this to specify Jupiter, and to show that Jupiter satisfied strong eventual consistency and the weak list specification. To our knowledge, their TLA+ definition of the weak list specification is the only other executable checker of this property, but it still does not cover the strong list specification or the non-interleaving properties.
Zhang et al.\ introduced the MET framework \cite{Zhang2024}, which uses TLC to model check strong eventual consistency in CRDTs specified in TLA+, and then reuses the traces that the checker explored as test cases in a real implementation.


Wei et al.\ \cite{Wei2018} also showed that Jupiter satisfied the weak list specification by proposing a new algorithm CJupiter. They showed CJupiter satisfied the weak list specification, and that the algorithm was equivalent to Jupiter.


Gomes et al.\ use the Isabelle/HOL proof assistant to prove strong eventual consistency for three CRDTs including RGA, but only for the convergence property \cite{Gomes2017}.

Replication Benchmarker \cite{Urso2016} is a collection of collaborative text editing algorithm implementations, but it is oriented towards benchmarking rather than model checking, and it covers far fewer algorithms than our corpus.

There are many algorithms that solve problems related to collaborative text editing: collaborative XML editing \cite{Davis2002, Martin2010report}, graphics editing \cite{Sun2002}, tree editing \cite{Molli2003, Jungnickel2015}, JSON editing \cite{Jungnickel2016, Kleppmann2017} and rich-text editing \cite{Litt2022} all apply these ideas to different kinds of documents, often using similar algorithms. At present, Darner only models plain text and properties that are well-defined on plain text, and we leave an extension to other document types to future work.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented Darner, a model checker specialised for testing properties of collaborative text editing algorithms. Apart from convergence and the weak list specification, the other properties we checked for (Attiya et al.'s strong list specification \cite{Attiya2016}, forward non-interleaving, and maximal non-interleaving \cite{Weidner2025Fugue}) did not previously have executable checkers, and this is the first time these properties have been checked against most collaborative text-editing algorithms. 
We provide a corpus of implementations covering most of the research literature and open-source libraries on collaborative text editing, and test these with Darner to produce a set of results documenting which algorithms satisfy which properties.
Our implementations resolve multiple ambiguities in previous research, and for many algorithms, ours is the first publicly available implementation. We find previously unreported convergence violations in four algorithms, violations of the weak list specification in six, and many violations of the other properties.
Darner supports both randomised and exhaustive checking, and we evaluate the behaviour of these two strategies, justifying our use of random over exhaustive checking. We hope Darner will help to quickly rule out broken algorithms, so future research in this area may avoid the correctness issues that have plagued this field in the past.

Future work may apply more rigorous proof techniques to verify the algorithms for which Darner is unable to find a violation. 
Also, our work does not check for deletions with non-interleaving; future work should investigate how the non-interleaving properties apply to algorithms that do not use tombstones. Our preliminary exploration of this is included in the repository.
Performance characteristics of these algorithms would also be interesting to study, though this is challenging as much depends on the data structure optimisations used by particular implementations \cite{Gentle2025}.

\section{Algorithm implementations} \label{sec:algorithmimplementations}

This section supplements \autoref{tab:algorithmproperties} with additional findings about specific algorithms.

\subsection{dOPT, Jupiter and adOPTed} \label{sec:doptjupiteradoptedimplementation}

dOPT \cite{Ellis1989} is the original OT algorithm, which is well known not to work. The transformation functions proposed in the same paper satisfy neither TP1 nor TP2. Furthermore, the algorithm has an issue which manifests because it calls its inclusion transformations with two operations that were not generated from the same state when two clients are out of sync by more than one operation. Similar issues are referred to in the literature as the dOPT puzzle \cite{Sun1998CSCW}. The original paper does mention the correctness issues and claims that the problem lies in the priority scheme, which decides which character should appear first when there are two concurrent insertions at the same location. The authors propose an alternative priority scheme, but this is also known not to work \cite{Sun1998CSCW}, so we do not implement it.

The Jupiter algorithm \cite{Nichols1995} is based on dOPT and uses a server, and claims to fix its issues. Darner finds that this algorithm converges and satisfies the weak list specification (which matches proofs of these properties \cite{Wei2018}). The paper does not fully specify transformation functions, so we use a set of transformation functions that were formally proven to satisfy TP1 \cite{Kleppmann2023}. The paper also does not fully specify the behaviour of the server, but this is clarified in later papers \cite{Xu2014}. We were able to satisfy forward non-interleaving with this algorithm by swapping the server and client roles in the transformation functions.

The adOPTed algorithm \cite{Ressel1996} is another algorithm based on dOPT. It does not use a server. It is a natural extension of the Jupiter algorithm to multiple clients, and requires TP2-satisfying functions. We use adOPTed as our algorithm for checking whether a function satisfies TP2, since it is proven to converge when the functions satisfy TP2 (and that result aligns with our checker). However, the transformation functions given in the same paper as adOPTed do not satisfy TP2, resulting in non-convergence.

\subsection{SOCT2, SOCT3 and SOCT4}

SOCT2 \cite{Suleiman1997} is an OT algorithm requiring TP2-satisfying functions. The paper gives transformation functions, but Darner finds these do not satisfy TP2 (or even TP1), as they lead to divergence with adOPTed with both two and three clients. That aligns with known results \cite{Oster2005Proving}. Previous open-source implementations \cite{Urso2016} have made the algorithm work using the tombstone transformation functions (TTF) \cite{Oster2006TTF}. We also find that this works. SOCT2 also requires functions satisfying the reversibility requirements (though they use the terms forward/backward transposition rather than inclusion/exclusion), which TTF claims to satisfy.


SOCT3 \cite{Vidot2000} is based on SOCT2, with the main change being that the delivery of operations is now serialised by introducing a sequencer (essentially a server). This means that SOCT3 is now TP2-avoiding, and claims to work with transformation functions that satisfy TP1 and the reversibility requirements, but not TP2. We could not find any functions that satisfy this specific set of conditions to test the algorithm with, so we tested it with the original SOCT2 transformation functions (which do not work), and the tombstone
transformation functions (which do work).


SOCT4 \cite{Vidot2000} further drops the reversibility requirements, so we found it works with the transformation functions we used for Jupiter, which only satisfy TP1. SOCT4 makes the assumption that all clients are online (and still uses a sequencer), which SOCT3 does not do.

\subsection{GOT and GOTO} \label{sec:gotimplementation}

The GOT \cite{Sun1998TOCHI} control algorithm claims to be a TP2-avoiding algorithm that does not use a server. We find this algorithm fails to converge with the transformation functions given in the same paper. The functions given in the paper do not satisfy the reversibility requirements they claim to satisfy. We experimented with implementing the algorithm using TTF, and were able to achieve convergence, but still could not satisfy the weak list specification. A full description of the issues with GOT is included alongside the implementation in the repository.

The GOTO \cite{Sun1998CSCW} algorithm is an optimised GOT algorithm that assumes TP2-satisfying functions. We believe the algorithm is equivalent to SOCT2, only differing in what names are given to the same concepts. Therefore, we use the same implementation for GOTO and SOCT2.

\subsection{TIBOT and TIBOT 2.0}

TIBOT \cite{Li2004ICPADS} and TIBOT 2.0 \cite{Xu2014} are both TP2-avoiding algorithms that claim to work without a server. However, both introduce a total order on the operations and have a time-interval mechanism for ensuring that operations are delivered in this total order. This makes the assumption that all clients are online.

We find that both algorithms work with the TP1 functions we used for Jupiter. Both algorithms deliver operations as a group rather than individually. However, the TIBOT 2.0 description does not fully describe how to handle these group operations. Fortunately, the machinery to handle this is all present in the TIBOT algorithm, so the description for single operations was easily translated to group operations in our implementation.


\subsection{POT and COT}

POT \cite{Xu2016} and COT \cite{Sun2009} are another pair of TP2-avoiding algorithms. They claim not to use a server, but they require operations to be delivered in the same total order on all clients, so we categorise them as total-order-based as per \autoref{sec:assumptions}.

Both algorithms work as expected. Although comparing performance is not a goal of this paper, we observe that the COT algorithm uses a very slow recursive algorithm --- too slow to model check even a few hundred cases. Memoisation (as suggested in the paper) was therefore necessary for model checking (and in any practical implementation).

\subsection{SDT, ABT and LBT}

SDT \cite{Li2004CSCW}, LBT \cite{Li2005} and ABT \cite{Li2010} are three algorithms proposed by the same authors, claiming to be TP2-avoiding and not to use a server. SDT is known not to work \cite{Oster2005Proving}, but LBT and ABT had not previously been independently evaluated. We found that LBT fails to converge, but ABT works with the transformation functions from the same paper. This makes ABT the only TP2-avoiding, causal-order-based OT algorithm we tested for which we could not find convergence or list specification violations. 

The LBT counterexample can be exhibited with two clients, initially with an empty state. Client 1 performs $O_1 = \mathit{ins}(a,0)$, then $O_2 = \mathit{del}(0)$.
Client 2 performs $O_3 = \mathit{ins}(c,0)$, then receives $O_1$, then performs $O_4=\mathit{del}(0)$. Finally, client 2 performs $O_5 = \mathit{ins}(f,1)$. Once all the remaining network messages are received, client 1 will have the state ``fc'', but client 2 will have the state ``cf''. That is a convergence violation. A full walkthrough of this trace is included in the repository.

\subsection{WOOT, WOOTO and WOOTH}

We found that the WOOT \cite{Oster2006WOOT} algorithm satisfies convergence and the weak and strong list specifications. Its successors, WOOTO \cite{Weiss2007} and WOOTH \cite{AhmedNacer2011}, both satisfy the same properties, the changes being performance improvements. WOOTH is not formally described in a paper, but given as open-source code.

\subsection{TM1.1 and Randolph functions} \label{sec:tm11andrandolphfunctions}

The TM1.1 transformation functions \cite{Sun2017} and Randolph et al.'s functions \cite{Randolph2015} both claim to satisfy TP2, but neither has been checked outside of its original paper. We implement both with the adOPTed algorithm, and find that both lead to convergence (including with more than two clients), indicating that they do indeed satisfy TP2. However, we find that with the TM1.1 functions, the algorithm does not satisfy the weak list specification, because it sometimes deletes a character that the user did not intend to delete.

The violation occurs when there is an insertion and a deletion to the same position. In this case, both characters are deleted rather than the character that was intended to be deleted. This behaviour violates condition 1a of the weak list specification.

In contrast, Darner could not find strong or weak list specification violations for Randolph et al.'s functions. The functions require comparing characters. Simply comparing character codes is not enough: it leads to convergence violations because it is not a total order, which the algorithm assumes it to be. Instead, we attach a client ID and a per-client counter to each character, which provides a total order; this is fairly common in CRDTs, but not as common in OT algorithms.

\subsection{Diffsync}

Darner's convergence results led us to identify a bug in Diffsync \cite{Little2017}: replicas could merge the same set of commits in different orders and obtain different document states. We fixed this by sorting the merge heads, a one-line change. Our evaluation uses the corrected version, which still exhibits weak list specification violations.

\subsection{Logoot}

The Logoot \cite{Weiss2009} algorithm is known to have bugs \cite{Sun2018}, which we were able to reproduce.
The algorithm has a BASE parameter, and the bugs occur when two random numbers in the range from 0 to $\textrm{BASE} - 1$ are equal.
The probability of violations thus depends on the choice of BASE; they are common when $\mathrm{BASE} = 2^4$, but did not occur in our tests with $\mathrm{BASE} = 2^{64}$.

The first bug leads to divergence; this bug was fixed in a later description of the algorithm \cite{Weiss2010}. The other causes the algorithm to get stuck in an infinite loop. Previous work has found that many of the fixes to this bug in open-source implementations \cite{Urso2016, Chen2017} introduce divergence bugs
elsewhere. We implemented both of those fixes and found they do not work. We were able to slightly modify one of these fixes \cite{Chen2017}, and with that, we were no longer able to produce violations of the weak or strong list specification.

\subsection{LogootSplit and LSEQ}

LogootSplit \cite{Andre2013} and LSEQ \cite{Nedelec2013} are two variants of Logoot. LSEQ is simply a change to the allocation strategy, so all the issues with Logoot apply to LSEQ as well. LogootSplit is a bigger change, and we found it does not have the same bugs as Logoot. LogootSplit has three variants --- LogootSplitNaive, LogootSplitString and LogootSplitAVL. We only implement LogootSplitNaive; the differences between the three are only in the data structures used to improve performance.

\subsection{Partial persistent sequences (PPS)} \label{sec:ppsimplementation}

A PPS \cite{Wu2010} represents positions using rational numbers between 0 and 1. When inserting a character between two other characters with positions $p$ and $q$, the position of the new character must be some value between $p$ and $q$. In the final state, the characters are ordered by this position. This idea was mentioned previously \cite{Preguica2009}, but dismissed because it requires arbitrary-precision rationals. Even without considering that issue, we find that the algorithm fails to converge because it does not give unique identifiers when two users concurrently insert at the same position. The two papers proposing this algorithm \cite{Wu2009, Wu2010} identify this issue and each provides a separate fix. We find that neither fix works entirely, and provide counterexamples.  

The first fix is, when inserting between $p$ and $q$, to split that range into subranges. For example, if $p=0$ and $q=1$, and there are 10 clients, client 1 will only insert between 0 and 0.1, client 2 will only insert between 0.1 and 0.2, and so on. The problem with this is that different clients may see different ranges, which leads to their subranges overlapping. Suppose the state is ``abc'' for client 2, but only ``ac'' for client 1 (because it has not yet received the insertion for `b'). Also, suppose `a' has position 0.1, `b' has position 0.5, and `c' has position 0.8. If both clients try to insert after `a', client 1 is inserting between 0.1 and 0.8, while client 2 is inserting between 0.1 and 0.5. This means the subranges that each client sees will be different, and could overlap, leading to collisions.


The second fix is to append digits to the end of the rational number. For example, if the client has ID 1 and is trying to insert at position $0.5$, the new position will be $0.51$. The problem is that it is not clear which part is the actual position and which is the client ID. With more than 10 clients, we found situations where this ambiguity meant two characters were assigned the same position.

\subsection{Text without CRDTs}

We implemented both the server-based and Lamport-ordered peer-to-peer variants described by Weidner \cite{Weidner2025without}. The CRDT row in \autoref{tab:algorithmproperties} refers to the peer-to-peer variant; both variants gave the same results for the properties checked. The blog post notes that the Lamport-ordered variant has the same list order as RGA, so its maximal non-interleaving violation is a known result.

\subsection{TreeDoc}

We found that the TreeDoc \cite{Preguica2009} algorithm satisfies the strong list specification. There is some ambiguity over what is meant by TreeDoc in the literature. The original paper
represents a document as a binary tree with major nodes and mini nodes. Other papers \cite{Zawirski2011} represent TreeDoc as a tree that is not binary. A node can have many right
children and many left children. Depending on the interpretation, this can lead to something closer to the Fugue algorithm \cite{Weidner2025Fugue}. The original paper also 
describes two kinds of ID scheme (UDIS and SDIS), each of which has a different way of correctly handling deletions. Our implementation uses major nodes and mini nodes faithful to the original description, and we used SDIS.

\subsection{RGA}

In the original RGA paper \cite{Roh2009}, positions were described using S4Vectors. Later descriptions of RGA do not use S4Vectors \cite{Shapiro2011report}. We implemented both, and found they satisfied the same properties. Timestamped insertion trees \cite{Attiya2016} and causal trees \cite{Grishchenko2010} are both different names for the same algorithm, equivalent to RGA. 

Kleppmann et al.~\cite{Kleppmann2019} proposed a fix to RGA to avoid interleaving behaviour. That fix was later shown to be incorrect \cite{Weidner2025Fugue}, as the algorithm no longer converged.

\subsection{Diamond Types and Eg-walker}

Our Diamond Types implementation follows the FugueMax right-origin rule, as does the pseudocode in the extended Eg-walker paper \cite{Gentle2025}. The TypeScript reference implementation linked from that paper\footnote{\url{https://github.com/josephg/eg-walker-reference}} instead uses Fugue's right-origin rule, with the FugueMax alternative commented out, although its README says its ordering should match YjsMod and FugueMax. The Diamond Types row in \autoref{tab:algorithmproperties} therefore uses FugueMax ordering.

\subsection{ProseMirror}

We ported the collaborative editing algorithm from ProseMirror \cite{Haverbeke2015} into our checker. We found that the algorithm exhibits weak list specification violations due to reordering characters in certain scenarios. We provide a reproduction of this violation, calling the original ProseMirror library, in our repository. In this example, the algorithm reorders a character, which the user initially inserted at the front of the document, to the back of the document.

\subsection{Loro} \label{sec:loroimplementation}

Loro's documentation \cite{Loro2026} lists ``Fugue / Maximal non-inter\-leaving'' as a supported feature. However, its text implementation uses Fugue's ordering rather than FugueMax's. We reproduced maximal non-interleaving violations in the published Loro library as well as our port. This is a known limitation of Fugue's ordering, so we do not mark the violation as new in \autoref{tab:algorithmproperties}.

\subsection{Yjs and YjsMod}

The convention in the Fugue paper is that the left origin cannot be a tombstone, but the right origin can. Both Yjs and YjsMod (as implemented in reference-crdts \cite{Gentle2021}) follow the opposite convention, where the left origin can be a tombstone, and the right origin cannot. In order to be consistent with the other algorithms, we switch this to the convention of the Fugue paper.

\subsection{Anchor-based CRDTs}

We find that some CRDTs (such as Fugue, FugueMax, RGA, Sync9, Yjs, YjsMod) have a similar design. We include in the repository an origin order checker, which is an investigation into the similarities and differences between the behaviours of these algorithms.
